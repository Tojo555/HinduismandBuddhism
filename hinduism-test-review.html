Question 1 (4 pts)

Explain what Arjuna’s dilemma in the Gita is, and what dharma has to do with it.

Check your answer against the completed notes

Setting

  • A battlefield just before a war between two sets of cousins: the Pandavas (the “dharma” side, led by five brothers including Arjuna) and the Kauravas (the “adharma” side).
  • Arjuna is the warrior prince. Krishna is his charioteer, and also the Supreme God.

The dilemma

  • Krishna halts the chariot between the two armies. Arjuna sees his kinsmen gathered against him and says, “I shall not fight” (2.9).
  • Dharma’s role: his individual dharma as a warrior says he must fight for a good cause and protect his kinsmen. But he also has a dharma not to kill his kinsmen, teachers, and others he should respect. Two duties collide. Key passage: 2.7.

Krishna’s answer

  • Fight, but with the right mindset.
  • Reincarnation: you are not killing anyone permanently (2.11–13, 2.17–22, 2.27).
  • Everyone’s atman is eternal (2.18, 2.20, 2.30).
  • It is Arjuna’s duty to fight (2.31–36).
  • Acting without selfish desire leads to moksha (2.51, 2.70–72).

Sample answer

Arjuna’s dilemma is that he has conflicting sacred duties. He even tells Krishna this in the opening stanzas of Chapter 2. On the one hand, as a member of the warrior class, his dharma as a warrior is to protect his brothers and kinsmen. But to do so, he has to kill his cousins and his teachers on the other side of the battle. He also has a duty as a relative to protect his relatives and teachers, and there is no way to protect his brothers without harming his other relatives and teachers.

Question 2 (4 pts)

Explain who Oppenheimer is, and why he was so drawn to Arjuna’s dilemma in the Gita.

Check your answer against the completed notes

Who he is

  • J. Robert Oppenheimer was an American physicist and the scientific director of the Los Alamos laboratory, the center of the Manhattan Project, the U.S. effort in World War II to build the atomic bomb. (The project as a whole was directed by General Leslie Groves.)
  • He had studied Sanskrit and read the Gita, which he called a deeply influential book.

Why the dilemma drew him

  • Like Arjuna, he was caught between competing duties: his duty to his country and his work in wartime, and the horror of what the weapon would do.
  • Krishna’s advice (do your duty, act without attachment to the results) gave him a way to think about acting when the outcome is terrible.
  • After the first test of the bomb, he recalled the line where Krishna reveals himself as Death, the destroyer of worlds.

Sample answer

Oppenheimer was the scientific director of the Los Alamos laboratory, the center of the Manhattan Project, which created the atomic bomb for the United States in World War II. He was drawn to Arjuna’s dilemma because he too had conflicting duties. He had a duty to his country, the US, to win the war. But he knew as a scientist that he also had a duty to humanity in general. Creating a weapon of mass destruction means killing a lot of innocent people. And on top of that, he was probably unsure whether he really was serving humanity in the long run by creating such a powerful weapon.

TEA paragraph (14 pts)

One of our enduring understandings: “Simple statements about any religion are misleading because every religion is complex and diverse.” Hinduism is an exemplar of that diversity because of how it accommodates the natural diversity of humanity.

Your task

In what ways does Hinduism accommodate the fact that people are different?

Explain three ways, using key terms along the way. Structure: Topic sentence, Evidence (your three ways), Analysis. Mechanics doesn’t matter.

Check your answer against the completed notes

Ways Hinduism accommodates different people

  • Four aims of life: kama (sensual pleasure), artha (wealth, fame, power), dharma (sacred duty), moksha (liberation from samsara). All are legitimate, though the later ones are seen as more selfless and longer-lasting.
  • Different yogas (paths to moksha): karma yoga (action), jnana yoga (wisdom: atman = brahman), bhakti yoga (devotion to a chosen god, the most popular path, open to women and the casteless).
  • Choice of gods: you can worship various gods as supreme, because devotional Hindus say Brahman has attributes and qualities.
  • Choice of forms: you can even choose among the various forms of those gods (see Gita 11.44).
  • Not a good answer: the caste system, because there is no choice involved.

Key terms you could use

kamaarthadharmamokshasamsarayogakarma yogajnanabhaktiatmanbrahmanavatar

Sample paragraph

Hinduism accommodates the fact that people are different in several ways. First, it recognizes four legitimate aims of life: kama (pleasure), artha (wealth, fame, and power), dharma (sacred duty), and moksha (liberation from samsara), so people who want different things can all pursue something valid. Second, it offers several yogas, or paths to moksha. Thoughtful, philosophical people can follow jnana yoga, the path of wisdom, by realizing that their atman is brahman, while people who prefer love and worship can follow bhakti yoga, the path of devotion to a chosen god, which is open to everyone. Third, Hinduism lets people choose which god to worship as supreme, and even which form of that god to focus on, because devotional Hindus believe Brahman has attributes. Together, these choices show that Hinduism does not expect everyone to reach the same goal in the same way.
