Describe a moment from today or yesterday when you suffered or felt dissatisfied. How could understanding or applying Buddhist ideas have reduced that suffering? (4 pts)

Minimum to unlock the sample: 60 words.

0 / 60 words

Sample answer

Yesterday I got really upset when my coach didn’t start me in our soccer game, and I barely talked to anyone at dinner. The second noble truth says suffering is caused by craving, and I was craving to be a starter and to be seen as one of the best players. If I had practiced Right View, I would have noticed that my idea that “starting = happiness” was the very thing making me unhappy; as Thich Nhat Hanh writes, “it is often our very idea of happiness that prevents us from being happy.” I also could have remembered impermanence: lineups change every week, and so would my mood. Finally, Right Speech would have stopped me from complaining about the coach to teammates, which only spread my frustration.

How this answer meets the question

  • Specific momentIt names one real event from yesterday, not a general habit like “I get stressed about sports.” The question asks about today or yesterday, so the example has to be that concrete.
  • Source of sufferingIt identifies the craving underneath the frustration. That links the moment to the second noble truth instead of just describing a bad day.
  • Buddhist conceptIt uses three Buddhist ideas (Right View, impermanence, and Right Speech), and each one is used accurately.
  • Suffering reducedEvery concept is followed by exactly what would have changed. This is the part that is easiest to skip: naming a concept is not enough unless you show how it would have reduced the suffering.

Relevant notes

  • Second noble truth: suffering is caused by craving or desire.
  • Right View: “Where there is perception, there is deception”; “Our perceptions carry with them all the errors of subjectivity” (Hanh 52–53).
  • Right Speech: speak truthfully, don’t speak with a forked tongue, don’t speak cruelly (Hanh 84–85).
  • Impermanence: everything changes, as the Four Sights showed Siddhartha.
  • The Middle Way: balance between hedonism and asceticism.

Write out the Four Noble Truths by heart, in their proper order. (4 pts)

Minimum to unlock the answer: 20 words. Number them 1–4.

0 / 20 words

The Four Noble Truths

1. Life involves suffering (dissatisfaction): sickness, old age, death, and not getting what we want.

2. Suffering is caused by craving, or desire: attachment to things that are impermanent.

3. Suffering can end. Its end is nirvana.

4. The way to end suffering is the Eightfold Path, the Middle Way.

How this answer meets the question

  • Key ideaEach truth has one highlighted idea that carries its meaning: suffering, its cause, its end, and the path. Compare your answer to these four first.
  • Proper order: the truths work like a doctor’s visit. First the illness (suffering), then its cause (craving), then the news that it can be cured (it can end), and finally the treatment (the Eightfold Path). If yours are out of order, the logic breaks.
  • By heart: the explanation after each key idea shows you understand the truth, not just the label. A short phrase like “suffering is caused by craving” is better than one word.

    Sample paragraph

    Topic sentenceArgumentEvidenceAnalysisHindu termBuddhist term

    Both Hinduism and Buddhism are diverse within themselves: Hinduism offers many paths and many gods, while Buddhism makes room for different ideals and different ways of teaching the same truths. As Prothero explains, Hindus largely agree that samsara is the problem and moksha is the solution, but they disagree about how to get there. A philosophically minded Hindu may follow jnana yoga, trying to see through maya until realizing that atman is Brahman, while roughly a billion Hindus follow bhakti yoga, devoting themselves to a chosen god such as Vishnu, Shiva, or Durga. Even the gods multiply: Vishnu alone has ten avatars, and Eck observes that for Hindus “manyness is valued; indeed, it is seen as essential.” This variety lets each Hindu choose the path and the god that fit them. Buddhism is diverse in its own way. Although all Buddhists share the Four Noble Truths, Mahayana Buddhists especially honor the bodhisattva, who seeks enlightenment out of compassion for all beings, as in the story of the Hungry Tigress. The Buddha’s dharma was also meant to be explained differently to different people; Hanh writes that “we can describe the truth in different ways to help different listeners understand.” In both traditions, then, diversity is not a weakness but a way of meeting people where they are.

    How this paragraph meets the rubric

    • Topic sentence (1 pt)It answers the question in one sentence and previews both arguments: many paths and gods for Hinduism, different ideals and ways of teaching for Buddhism.
    • Two distinctive arguments (6 pts)One claim about Hinduism, one about Buddhism. Notice that neither one compares the religions; each shows variety within one tradition.
    • EvidenceEach argument is backed by specifics: the yogas, the gods and avatars, the bodhisattva, and quotations from Prothero, Eck, and Hanh.
    • AnalysisShort sentences explain why the evidence matters, rather than letting the facts speak for themselves.
    • Terms (4 pts)Far more than four review-sheet terms, each used in a way that shows what it means. Saffron terms are Hindu; maroon terms are Buddhist.

    Relevant notes

    • “Hinduism is the least dogmatic and the most diverse” (Prothero 134).
    • Three yogas: karma (action), jnana (wisdom), bhakti (devotion); bhakti is by far the most popular.
    • Four aims of life: kama, artha, dharma, moksha.
    • Many gods, many forms: Vishnu’s ten avatars; Devi as Durga or Kali; Shiva in many forms.
    • Eck: Mount Meru is wider at the top than the bottom, making room for many “best” things.
    • Buddhism: the bodhisattva ideal and emptiness in Mahayana; Right Speech allows describing truth “in different ways to help different listeners.”
    • Remember: the caste system is a weak example here, because no choice is involved.

      In both Hinduism and Buddhism, enlightenment comes from seeing the self as it truly is: Hindus discover that the self is one with the divine, while Buddhists discover that there is no fixed self at all. In philosophical Hinduism, knowing the self means seeing through illusion. Maya makes the world look like a collection of separate things, but a seeker on the path of jnana yoga learns that their atman, the individual self, is identical to Brahman, the universal self. Once a person truly knows this, there is no reason to act selfishly, so they gather no karma and escape samsara to reach moksha. The Gita makes the same point when Krishna teaches that desire is what “obscures knowledge,” suggesting that the true battlefield is inside our own minds. Buddhism also turns inward. After teachers and asceticism failed him, the Buddha decided he “would look within and trust himself.” What he found was nonself: as Hanh puts it, “Non-self means that you are made of elements that are not you.” Practicing Right View means recognizing that “where there is perception, there is deception,” much like the confirmation bias we studied at the start of the course. In both religions, then, knowing the self is not just useful self-awareness; it is the very doorway to moksha and nirvana.

      How this paragraph meets the rubric

      • Topic sentence (1 pt)It answers the question and previews both arguments: for Hindus the self is one with the divine; for Buddhists there is no fixed self.
      • Two distinctive arguments (6 pts)One on Hinduism, one on Buddhism, as the question requires. Both stay focused on knowing the self, not on knowing others.
      • EvidenceSpecific ideas and quotations: atman and Brahman, the Gita on desire, the Buddha looking within, and Hanh on nonself.
      • AnalysisEach piece of evidence is followed by what it means: why knowing the self leads to moksha, and why Right View connects to the bias we studied.
      • Terms (4 pts)Many review-sheet terms from both religions, plus a critical-thinking term, each used accurately.

      Relevant notes

      • Enlightenment in the “Eastern” religions is usually the pursuit of “full and true knowledge of the self.”
      • Enduring understanding 1: “Being aware of your own biases, biased perspectives, and inborn mental shortcomings is a big step towards ‘enlightenment.’”
      • Philosophical Hinduism: see through maya; atman = Brahman; selflessness means no karma, which leads to moksha.
      • Gita 3.36–43: desire causes evil and obscures knowledge; the true battlefield may be the mind.
      • The Buddha “would look within and trust himself” (echoes jnana yoga and maya).
      • Nonself (Hanh 135); Baggini: “you are the sum of your parts.” Emptiness extends nonself to all things.
      • Right View: “Where there is perception, there is deception” (Hanh 52–53).

      Step 1: Memorize the definition

      Write our class definition of myth from memory. It will NOT be given to you on the test.

      Minimum to unlock the answer: 10 words.

      0 / 10 words

      The definition

      Myth: a sacred, often symbolic story that explains or establishes rituals, ethics, and worldviews of a society.

      Which parts did you include?

        A simpler version to help it stick: myths are stories that tell believers how to act (rituals and ethics) and what to believe (worldviews).

        Key terms inside the definition

        • Ritual: an established procedure that may or may not be religious (puja, darshan, meditation).
        • Ethics: an area of study that deals with ideas about what is good and bad behavior.
        • Worldview: a set of fundamental beliefs, values, etc., constituting a comprehensive outlook on the world; a perspective on life.

        Step 2: Study a worked example

        Carrying the Woman Across the River

        This first story is real. It comes from the Zen Buddhist tradition, where it is often taught as a koan: a short story, question, or saying that Zen teachers use to jolt students out of ordinary thinking and toward insight. Some koans are puzzling on purpose, like “What is the sound of one hand clapping?” Others, like this one, end with a line that stops you in your tracks.

        Two monks, Tanzan and Ekido, were walking together down a muddy road after a heavy rain. At a river crossing they came upon a young woman in a fine silk kimono, unable to get across without ruining it. Without a word, Tanzan picked her up, carried her through the mud and water, and set her down on the other side. The two monks walked on in silence. Monks of their order were not supposed to touch women, and Ekido grew more and more troubled. Hours later, when they reached the temple that night, he could hold it in no longer. “We monks are not supposed to go near women,” he burst out, “especially young and lovely ones. Why did you do that?” Tanzan replied, “I set her down hours ago, back at the river. Are you still carrying her?”

        Sample answer

        Term or idea (1 pt each)Which part of the myth definitionSpecific moment in the storyExplanation

        This story functions as a Buddhist myth because it establishes a worldview about where suffering comes from and an ethic of letting go. First, it expresses the Second Noble Truth, the worldview that suffering is caused by craving and attachment. Hours after the crossing, Ekido is still upset, and Tanzan asks, “Are you still carrying her?” Ekido’s suffering does not come from the woman or from Tanzan; it comes from his own clinging to that moment and to the rule. Tanzan let go, so he is at peace. Second, the story teaches impermanence as an ethic for how to live. The crossing lasted only a few minutes, and Tanzan treated it that way, setting the woman down and moving on. Because every moment passes, Buddhists learn that the right way to act is to help when help is needed and then let the moment go, instead of carrying it for hours.

        How this answer earns all 6 points

        • Two terms (2 pts)The Second Noble Truth and impermanence are both clearly present in the story, and each is named directly.
        • Ties to the definitionEach term is connected to a specific part of our definition: one is a worldview, the other is an ethic. That connection is what turns a summary into a myth analysis.
        • Story evidenceEach point refers to a specific moment, including Tanzan’s final line, so the reader can see the term at work.
        • Explanation (2 pts each)Each point explains what a Buddhist reader learns about how to live or what to believe, not just what happened in the story.

        Other ideas that would also work

        • Right View: Ekido’s perception is distorted by his rigid idea of the rule; Tanzan sees a person who needs help.
        • The Middle Way: Tanzan neither breaks the spirit of his vows nor clings to the letter of the rule.
        • Nonself or compassion: Tanzan acts without thinking about how it makes him look.

        Practice story 1 (Hindu): The Potter’s Lamp

        In a village on the banks of the Ganges lived a potter named Madhav, so poor that he could never afford the silks and gold that wealthy families offered at the temple of Krishna. Each morning before work, he shaped a small clay lamp, filled it with a few drops of oil, and set it before the image of Krishna with a quiet prayer: “Everything I make today, I make for you.” Then he went to his wheel and worked all day, never asking for anything in return. One festival day, a rich merchant arrived with a cart of golden offerings, making sure the whole village saw him. That night the temple priest dreamed that Krishna appeared to him, smiling. “The merchant gave me gold,” Krishna said, “but he was thinking of his own name. Madhav gives me a lamp of clay, and his whole heart comes with it. His is the lamp that lights my temple.” When Madhav died many years later, the villagers said he was never born again, for Krishna had taken him home.

        How does this story function as a myth according to our definition of myth?

        Term or ideaWhich part of the myth definitionSpecific moment in the storyExplanation

        This story functions as a Hindu myth by establishing both a ritual and an ethic. First, it models bhakti yoga through the ritual of puja, making an offering to the image of a god. Every morning Madhav sets a clay lamp before Krishna, and Krishna says it is the lamp that lights his temple. The story teaches Hindus that what matters in worship is love and devotion, not how expensive the offering is. Second, it teaches selfless action leading to moksha as an ethic, along with the worldview behind it. The merchant gives to be seen, while Madhav works “for you” and asks for nothing; in the end he is “never born again.” Because Madhav acts without selfish desire, he gathers no karma, and his god grants him liberation from samsara, showing believers how to act and what they can hope for.

        Other ideas that would also work

        • Karma: the merchant’s selfish giving versus Madhav’s selfless giving.
        • Dharma: Madhav keeps doing his duty as a potter, offering it to his god.
        • Avatar: Krishna, the god Madhav worships, is an avatar of Vishnu.

        Practice story 2 (Hindu): The Wave and the Ocean

        A young prince named Devesh could not stop weeping after his grandmother died. “She is gone,” he told his teacher, an old sage who lived by the sea. “Everything she was has ended.” The sage led him down to the shore and pointed at the water. “Watch that wave,” he said. A wave rose, curled, and crashed against the sand. “Is it gone?” Devesh nodded. “Look again,” said the sage. The water slid back into the sea, and a moment later another wave rose in its place. “The wave was never separate from the ocean,” the sage said. “It only looked that way. Your grandmother’s body has fallen, as a wave falls, but the self inside her was never born and can never die. It will rise again in another form. And one day, when it stops believing it is only a wave, it will know that it was the ocean all along.” Devesh wiped his eyes and sat by the water until the sun went down.

        How does this story function as a myth according to our definition of myth?

        Term or ideaWhich part of the myth definitionSpecific moment in the storyExplanation

        This story functions as a Hindu myth because it establishes two central Hindu worldviews. First, it expresses samsara, the worldview that every self is reborn again and again. The sage says the grandmother’s self “will rise again in another form,” just as another wave rises after the first one falls. This teaches Hindus not to see death as an ending, which is why Devesh can stop grieving, much like Krishna tells Arjuna not to grieve in the Gita. Second, it teaches that atman is Brahman, the worldview at the heart of philosophical Hinduism. The wave “was never separate from the ocean; it only looked that way,” and one day it will know “it was the ocean all along.” The wave stands for the individual self and the ocean for the universal self. Seeing past the illusion of separateness is how a person finally reaches moksha.

        Other ideas that would also work

        • Maya: the wave only “looked” separate, the illusion of difference.
        • Moksha: knowing it was “the ocean all along” is liberation from the cycle.
        • Jnana yoga: the sage teaches through wisdom and understanding, not devotion.

        Practice story 3 (Buddhist): The Sand Mandala

        For three weeks, the young monk Tenzin and four older monks bent over a wooden platform in the monastery hall, tapping colored sand grain by grain from thin metal funnels. Slowly a mandala appeared: circles within squares within circles, gates and lotus petals in red, gold, and blue, so detailed that visitors held their breath. Tenzin had never made anything so beautiful. On the final morning, the abbot chanted a prayer, then drew his hand straight through the center of the design. The other monks swept the colors into a single gray pile. Tenzin felt his throat tighten. “Three weeks,” he whispered. “Gone.” The abbot poured the sand into an urn, and the monks walked in a line to the river, where they let it pour into the current. “It was always going to be swept away,” the abbot said gently. “So is this river. So is this body. So is your sadness. We did not make the mandala to keep it. We made it to see this.” Tenzin watched the colored water drift downstream until he could not tell it from the rest of the river.

        How does this story function as a myth according to our definition of myth?

        Term or ideaWhich part of the myth definitionSpecific moment in the storyExplanation

        This story functions as a Buddhist myth because it explains a ritual and the worldview behind it. First, it teaches impermanence by explaining the ritual of making and destroying a sand mandala. After three weeks of work, the abbot sweeps the mandala away and says, “We did not make the mandala to keep it. We made it to see this.” The story tells Buddhists why the ritual exists: destroying something beautiful is a way of practicing the truth that nothing lasts. Second, it expresses the Second Noble Truth, the worldview that suffering comes from craving and attachment. Tenzin’s throat tightens and he whispers, “Gone,” but by the end he watches the sand drift away calmly. His pain came from wanting to keep the mandala forever. When he lets go of that craving, his suffering eases, which is exactly what Buddhists believe is possible for everyone.

        Other ideas that would also work

        • Nonself: “So is this body” suggests even the self is temporary and made of changing parts.
        • Sangha: the monks work and walk together as a community.
        • Right View: seeing the mandala, the river, and sadness as they really are.

        Sand mandalas are a real Tibetan Buddhist ritual; this particular story was invented for practice.

        Practice story 4 (Buddhist): The Two Brothers and the Lute

        When a wealthy merchant died, he left his fortune to his two sons. The elder son spent his share on feasts, fine clothes, and wine, sleeping until noon and chasing every pleasure he could buy. The younger son, disgusted by his brother, gave away everything he owned and went into the forest, where he ate one handful of rice a day and slept on stones, believing that the more he suffered, the holier he would become. A year later both brothers were miserable: the elder was sick and in debt, and the younger was so weak he could hardly stand. A wandering nun found them resting at the same well. She took out a lute and plucked a string wound so tight that it snapped. Then she plucked one so loose that it made no sound at all. Finally she tuned a string just right, and a clear note rang out. “Your lives are like these strings,” she said. “One of you is too loose, and one of you is too tight. Neither of you can make music.” The brothers followed her back to her community of monks and nuns, where they learned to eat enough, rest enough, and sit in meditation each day.

        How does this story function as a myth according to our definition of myth?

        Other ideas that would also work

        • The Buddha’s life story: the brothers repeat his journey from palace to forest to balance.
        • The Second Noble Truth: both brothers suffer because of craving, one for pleasure and one for holiness.
